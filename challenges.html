Rules

These challenges are optional, ungraded and just for fun. They do not affect your grade, nothing is awarded, and you can skip them entirely. They are a chance to play with the ideas from the lectures: push an algorithm to its worst case, find where a clever shortcut fails, and race the class for the shortest delivery route.

How it works

  • Every exercise session has its own page on the course’s challenge site, with three things to do during the week: a main challenge with a live leaderboard, a handful of badges (short puzzles with one exact answer) and the session’s Kattis problems. In the session itself your table plays a short sprint.
  • You hand in on the site: paste your answer, press Submit, and the checker answers at once: valid or not (and why), your score and your rank. Resubmit as often as you like; your best valid answer counts.
  • Nicknames only. Pick a nickname (3-20 letters, digits, _ or -) that is not your real name. Your first submission gives you a personal key; the site remembers it in this browser. Save it somewhere: you need it on another device, and nobody else can post under your nickname without it.
  • You submit inputs, not programs: numbers, words, points or a route. The checker runs the fixed algorithm described below on your input. Write any helper programs you like, in any language, to find good inputs.
  • Use any tools you like, including AI tools. The fun is in understanding why your answer works; be ready to explain it if you share it in class.
  • The leaderboard is shown at the start of the next session, where someone explains the idea behind a top answer. The delivery-route challenge runs for most of the course, from Lecture 5 to the last exercise session.

E1 · Make Euclid work hardest (two parts)

Euclid’s algorithm (Levitin 1.1) computes gcd(m, n) by repeating r ← m mod n; m ← n; n ← r while n ≠ 0. Which pair of numbers makes it divide most often?

Part 1 (small numbers). Find the pair with 1 ≤ n ≤ m ≤ 100 that needs the most divisions. There are only 5050 pairs: a short program that tries them all, counting divisions as in Lecture 1, finds the answer in a blink. Submit m n in the part 1 form below.

Part 2 (huge numbers). The same question with 1 ≤ n ≤ m ≤ 1 000 000 000. Now there are about 5 · 10¹⁷ pairs; trying them all would take longer than anyone has. You already know where to look: Lectures 1 and 2 measured that consecutive Fibonacci numbers make Euclid work hardest. The challenge is to apply that exactly. Which consecutive pair is the largest that still fits under the bound, and how many divisions does it take? Check your part 1 answer first: is it a consecutive Fibonacci pair, and what are all the quotients along the way? (Lecture 1’s break pair (832040, 514229) is valid here, but far from the best.) Submit m n in the part 2 form.

Score: the number of mod operations Euclid performs. Higher is better. Each part has its own leaderboard.

This is the course in miniature: part 1 is solved by computing, part 2 by applying a pattern exactly where computing is hopeless. Lecture 3 proves that no pair below the bound beats the best Fibonacci pair.

E2 · The slowest search

Brute-force string matching (Levitin 3.2) tries every shift of the pattern along the text and compares characters from left to right until a mismatch. The checker counts every character comparison over all shifts.

Submit: two lines: a text of exactly 1000 characters and a pattern of exactly 10 characters, both using only a and b. The pattern must occur exactly once in the text. Score: the total number of character comparisons. Higher is better. Can you reach the worst case from the lecture?

E3 · The tallest tree and the bushiest tree

Insert the keys one at a time, in your order, into an empty binary search tree (Levitin 4.5). Inserting a key at depth d costs d comparisons.

Submit: a first line board: tall or board: bushy, then the numbers 1 to 1023, each exactly once, in any order (separated by spaces or line breaks). Score: the total number of comparisons. There are two leaderboards: the tallest tree (highest total) and the bushiest tree (lowest total). Hand in once in each form if you want to try both.

E4 · Jam the hash table

Each word gets a hash value by Horner’s rule (Levitin 6.5), with a = 1, b = 2, …, z = 26 and base 31, taken mod 101: h(w) = ((…((c₁ · 31 + c₂) · 31 + c₃) …) · 31 + cₖ) mod 101. The words are inserted in your order into an empty table of 101 cells with linear probing (Levitin 7.3): if a cell is taken, try the next one, wrapping around. Every cell inspected counts as one probe.

Submit: exactly 50 different words of 1-12 lowercase letters (a-z). Score: the total number of probes for all 50 insertions. Higher is better.

E5 · Coins that fool greedy

Greedy change-making always takes the largest coin that fits. For the coins 1, 5, 10 and 25 this is always optimal; for 1, 3 and 4 it is not (6 = 4 + 1 + 1 by greedy, but 3 + 3 uses only two coins).

Submit: 3 to 6 different coin values between 1 and 100, including 1. Score: the number of amounts from 1 to 200 for which greedy uses more coins than the optimum (found by the dynamic-programming algorithm of Levitin 8.1). Higher is better; ties are broken by the total number of extra coins.

E6 · Fool the nearest neighbour

The nearest-neighbour heuristic for the travelling salesman problem (Levitin 12.3) starts at the first point and always goes to the closest unvisited point (ties go to the point listed first), then returns to the start.

Submit: 10 different points with whole-number coordinates from 0 to 100, as 20 numbers x₁ y₁ x₂ y₂ .... The first point is the start. Score: the length of the nearest-neighbour tour divided by the length of the shortest possible tour. Higher is better.

The delivery-route challenge (Lecture 5 to the last exercise session)

The parcel service has a depot and 200 drops in a synthetic area of about 20 × 14 km. The coordinates are in the file routing-instance.txt: one line per point, id x y, where id 0 is the depot. Distances are straight lines (Euclidean).

Submit: the order in which to visit the drops: the numbers 1 to 200, each exactly once. The route starts and ends at the depot. Score: the total route length in km. Lower is better.

Two reference numbers are published with the instance: a lower bound (no route can be shorter than the minimum spanning tree of the points, a fact you will meet in Lectures 12 and 15) and the length of the nearest-neighbour route. The leaderboard also shows each route’s gap to the lower bound. Anything goes: exhaustive search on pieces, greedy ideas, local improvements, your own heuristics. In the last exercise session, volunteers can show how they built their route.

Practice problems on Kattis

For classic “write a program and let a judge test it” practice, each session has a contest on open.kattis.com with its problems. A Kattis account is optional; if you create one, you may use a nickname. Join the session’s contest and give your Kattis name once on the site’s “My nickname” page: your solves then appear on the season leaderboard (updated once a day).
